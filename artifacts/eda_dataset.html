Polyester Degrader Dataset

What counts as a positive, what counts as a negative, and every polyester substrate behind the positive label — with the bond each enzyme has to cut.

Positive

An enzyme experimentally reported to degrade a plastic whose backbone is joined by ester bonds.

That is the whole criterion. A polyester is a polymer whose chain is held together by the ester linkage –C(=O)–O–, and degrading it means hydrolysing that bond so the chain breaks. Every record comes from PAZy or PlasticDB, which document measured activity on a named material.

388 positives — 340 with verified activity, 48 reported but not independently confirmed. All 388 act on at least one of the 21 substrates below.

What is not in the positive set, and why

Plastics are excluded when their backbone is not joined by a carboxylic ester. The reason matters, and it is not the same reason in every row.

plasticbackbone bondwhy it is out
polyamide — nylonamide –C(=O)–NH– hydrolysable, but by an amidase — a different catalytic machine

polycarbonatecarbonate –O–C(=O)–O– an ester, but not a carboxylic one; few enzymes act on it

rubberC–C / C=C same — nothing for a hydrolase to cut

Why the ester is the bond that works

An ester's carbonyl carbon is electrophilic and its leaving group is good, so a serine nucleophile attacks it with a modest energy barrier. In an amide, the nitrogen lone pair delocalises into the carbonyl and gives the C–N bond partial double-bond character, raising that barrier by orders of magnitude for the uncatalysed reaction. In a C–C backbone there is no polarised bond and no heteroatom at all, so hydrolysis is not chemically available — degradation needs radical chemistry with cofactors and oxygen.

There is also an evolutionary reason. Cutin, the waxy polymer of plant cuticle, is a natural polyester, so cutinases already existed when PET was invented in 1941. PET's ester is close enough that those enzymes partly work on it. Nothing in nature ever resembled polyethylene, and nothing evolved to eat it.

These plastics are not excluded for being unbreakable. The source databases contain 65 polyamide-degrading and 15 polyurethane-degrading enzymes, and 92 of the 118 excluded entries have experimentally verified activity. Nylonases and polyurethanases are real. They are excluded because a different bond needs a different catalytic machine, so pooling them with PETases would mean training one classifier on unrelated enzyme families and calling the mixture a plastic degrader. Only the polyolefins and rubber are excluded on chemistry alone.

Negative

A protein never reported to degrade any plastic — drawn from UniProt and graded by how closely it resembles a polyester hydrolase.

Every negative label is an assumption of inactivity, not a measurement. None of the 423 was assayed on a plastic. What makes the set useful is the grading: resemblance was measured by fold and by catalytic machinery, not by enzyme class.

Only the first row is a hard negative. Those 51 proteins carry the same fold and the same catalytic triad as the positives and have simply never been reported to touch a plastic. Separating them is the actual task; the other 372 are separated trivially.

One hard negative is in validation. Validation's 76 negatives are 40 tRNA hydrolases, 35 naive controls and 1 genuine esterase, because the tRNA family forms three homology components and identity-bounded splitting sent one of them wholly to val. Any threshold or model tuned on validation was tuned against the easy task.

The two numbers that bound every claim

51 hard negatives, one of them in validation. And 66% of the positives are a single homology component — BPS0190, 256 of 388, with 74 components in all. Any per-protein confidence interval counts that one family 256 times, so intervals on this dataset must be bootstrapped over the homology component, never over proteins.

Together they are why the headline test AU-ROC of 0.9681 against all negatives becomes 0.8167 against the 10 fold-matched negatives in test, with a 95% interval reaching down to 0.676.
