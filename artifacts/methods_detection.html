Materials and methods · detection half

Detecting Polyester-Degrading Enzymes

How the dataset was built, how the ESM-2 650M probe and the BLASTp baseline were trained and compared, what the results were, and what is and is not supported by them. The mechanistic half — which SAE features the decision rests on — is a separate page.

01

The question, stated precisely

The label reads "does this enzyme degrade a polyester" — a substrate criterion. Two further constraints are applied in building the set, and naming all three is what makes the task honest:

Is this an α/β-hydrolase-fold enzyme that hydrolyses a polyester plastic?

constraintkeptremoved
substrate aromatic polyester (PET, PEF); aromatic–aliphatic copolyester (PBAT, PBSeT, Ecovio-FT); aliphatic polyester (PLA, PCL, PBS, PES, PBSA, PEA, PPL) 109 — polyamide 65, polyurethane 15, polyolefin/vinyl 13, rubber 8, polycarbonate 4, polyether 2, unknown 2
foldesterase / α-β-hydrolase 8 — proteases 6, chitinases 2
primary substrate classany polyester, PHA included 36 PHA depolymerases set aside, carrying no claim

Substrate names in full: PET polyethylene terephthalate · PEF polyethylene furanoate · PBAT poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) · PBSeT poly(butylene sebacate-co-terephthalate) · PLA polylactic acid · PCL polycaprolactone · PBS polybutylene succinate · PES polyethylene succinate (not polyethersulfone) · PBSA poly(butylene succinate-co-adipate) · PEA polyethylene adipate · PPL poly(β-propiolactone).

The third row carries no claim. 36 PHA depolymerases are set aside and simply unused — they are not a generalisation test. They would qualify as positives under the substrate rule, and the positive set already contains 25 enzymes that degrade a PHA, so nothing about them is unseen. The kept positives are also not one lineage: they span 74 homology components.
The fold constraint is not in the task name. Eight excluded enzymes do degrade a polyester — five are experimentally verified PLA-degrading proteases. They were removed because they have a subtilisin fold, not an α/β-hydrolase fold. They are precisely the cases that would test whether the model tracks polyester activity or tracks the fold. Eight sequences across two folds cannot train anything, so excluding them is the pragmatic call — but it narrows the task and should be stated.

Why polyesters, when they are not the most common plastic. By volume, polyethylene is ~30% of production, polypropylene ~19%, PET ~7%. The case is tractability, not volume: PE, PP, PVC and PS have carbon–carbon backbones with no bond a hydrolase can cleave. The ester bond is the exception, polyester is over half of global fibre production, and enzymatic PET recycling already operates industrially on this exact enzyme family.

02

Dataset assembly

PAZy + PlasticDB→ merge, dedup→ 542 rows→ exclusions→ 388 positives

Positives come from PAZy and PlasticDB only — 232 PAZy-only, 210 in both, 100 PlasticDB-only. Every row carries a traceable source_ids.

Negatives come from UniProt only, in two tiers:

tierquerynrole
3 · fold-matchedec:3.1.1.- AND reviewed 185hard — same subclass as PETase
4 · naive controlreviewed NOT ec:3.1.* 238easy — outside EC 3.1

ec:3.1.1.- is the exact-subclass form. The loose ec:3.1.1.* is a string prefix that also matches 3.1.10–3.1.14 (nucleases) — 6,174 reviewed entries against 4,294, so 1,880 would have been spurious.

Keeping plastizymes out of the negatives

EC 3.1.1 contains PETase (3.1.1.101), MHETase (3.1.1.102) and cutinase (3.1.1.74), so the query asks for them too. Three filters remove them: accession exclusion against the positive set; a name/keyword/EC regex (PETase|cutinase|terephthalate|plastic|…); and removal of anything ≥90% identical to a positive during clustering.

Verified against live UniProt, not assumed. All 72 reviewed entries carrying a plastic-degrading EC code were re-queried: 10/10 PET hydrolases, 1/1 MHETase, 61/61 cutinases are caught. 0 of the 423 delivered negatives carry any of those codes.
Known fragility. Only 152 of 388 positives have a UniProt accession, so for 60% of them the name filter is the only barrier. It holds because UniProt names these enzymes informatively — but the regex matched names, not EC codes. The EC codes have since been added to the pattern.

Phylum quota

The PET class is 72.6% Actinomycetota. Without matching, a probe could score well by detecting phylum rather than function. Negatives were drawn per-phylum to the positives' distribution. Tier 3 fell short — 213 of 240 — because UniProt holds only 174 reviewed Actinomycetota EC 3.1.1 entries against a quota of 177.

Limitation found later. That shortfall forced the tier to take whatever passed the EC filter, and EC 3.1.1 is a reaction class, not a fold. The tier labelled "fold-matched" is 27% fold-matched: only 50 of its 185 members carry the α/β hydrolase fold (InterPro IPR029058), confirmed independently by Pfam.

Label asymmetry. A positive means somebody measured degradation. A negative means nobody has reported it — an assumption, never an assay. 48 of the 388 positives are unverified (29 of 68 in 2_other_polyester), and 29 of those 48 are extrapolated-only, i.e. the label was inferred and never directly assayed.

Positive homology density bounds everything. The 388 modelled positives collapse into 74 homology components and the largest, BPS0190, holds 256 of them — 66%. Any per-protein metric counts that one cutinase family 256 times, so confidence intervals on this dataset must be bootstrapped over the component column, never over proteins.
03

Homology-aware splitting

mmseqs2 all-vs-all→ global NW realign→ union-find components→ stratified assignment

Pairs are prefiltered with mmseqs2, then realigned globally (Needleman–Wunsch, BLOSUM62, free terminal gaps, identity = matches / shorter length) because mmseqs reports local identity and would understate relatedness. Components are connected components under a 40% identity edge; whole components are assigned to splits, largest first, to whichever split is furthest below target across the classes it contains.

splitpositivesnegatives totalprevalence
train303285 5880.515
val4376 1190.361

Verified bound: max cross-split identity 0.3967 train/val, 0.3800 train/test, 0.3983 val/test.

A 30% bound was tested and rejected. It is achievable (max 0.2996) but makes the benchmark easier: a negative 40% identical to a positive must join that positive's component and therefore its split, so tightening the bound evicts the hard negatives from test. Hard negatives in test fall from 8 to 1, test positives from 42 to 25, and all six negatives that BLASTp ranks above its own median positive move into validation.
Effective sample size is far below the sequence count. The 42 test positives occupy only 20 homology components — Kish effective n 10.6, giving a 95% CI half-width on recall of ±18 pp. Cluster k-fold CV does not fix this: one component holds 256 of 303 training positives, so folds are degenerate (tested — two of five folds produce no metric at all).

05

Embeddings and the linear probe

ESM-2 650M (facebook/esm2_t33_650M_UR50D) via HuggingFace transformers. Residue embeddings from layers 1/9/18/24/30/33, pooled both mean and max — twelve representations, each 1,280-dimensional. Pooling is recorded in the output path because it materially changes performance.

settingvalue
classifierSGDClassifier, log_loss, L2
regularizationC = 1e-02, alpha = 1/(C·n_train)
learning rateeta0 = 1e-04, constant, 5000 epochs, tol=None
preprocessingStandardScaler, fitted on train, persisted
selected onval log-loss, using max_L33 only

Hyperparameters were swept over C × eta0 and chosen on max_L33 alone, then applied unchanged to all twelve representations.

Selection used log-loss, not AU-PRC, and that mattered. Val AU-PRC saturates — several cells tie at 0.9995 and one reaches a perfect 1.0000 at C=1, eta=1e-1, which is the corner where log-loss is 2.72: wildly miscalibrated and not converged. AU-PRC would have selected a broken model. Log-loss is a proper scoring rule and does not saturate.

Best representation: max_L33, val AU-ROC 0.9976 ± 0.0000 across five seeds.

06

BLASTp baseline — matched protocol

The database holds only the 303 training positives, so BLASTp gets exactly the information the probe was trained on. Each query keeps its single best hit; the score is that hit's e-value.

  1. Sweep the e-value cutoff on validation.
  2. Carry the chosen cutoff — e ≤ 3.16e-2 — to test unchanged.
An earlier version tuned BLASTp's cutoff on test, giving F1 0.820. Carrying the validation threshold honestly gives 0.598 on the PET task — an inflation of 0.22. Both methods now select their operating point on validation only.

08

Observations

The two methods tie at the operating point and separate in the curve

Both make exactly 8 false positives on test. BLASTp catches one more true positive, so its precision denominator is 48 rather than 47 — the 0.004 difference is arithmetic, not a difference in error behaviour. The separation is in the ranking.

Why BLASTp's test curve is a flat shelf

Its curve sits at ~0.84 precision from recall 0.05 to 0.95 with no high-precision region. Walking down its ranking explains it:

ranksequencetruthe-value running precision
1BPS0080PET hydrolase 4e-791.000
2P86325esterase 1e-740.500
3Q47M62esterase 3e-740.333
4BPS0079PET hydrolase 3e-740.500
5Q01470esterase 4e-550.400

BLASTp's second- and third-best hits in the entire test split are non-degraders. Once a false positive occupies rank 2, precision can never recover — tightening the cutoff removes true positives from the bottom long before it reaches these three. Six of 62 test negatives beat BLASTp's own median positive.

P86325 and BPS0080 match the same training protein (BPS0004) at 35% and 36% identity, e=1e-74 and 4e-79. One degrades polyester, one does not. No e-value cutoff separates them. The probe scores them 0.525 and 0.652 — barely, but in the right order.

Validation and test are not equally hard

Test drew markedly harder negatives: 8 of 62 clear BLASTp's cutoff against 2 of 76 in validation. Test positives are actually closer to the database (median best e-value 3.7e-15 vs 1e-09). Part of the measured gap therefore reflects which negatives happened to land in test, and the paired bootstrap resamples within test so cannot account for it.

The structural reason, found later. The 99 tRNA-processing negatives form only three homology components, and identity-bounded splitting keeps a component intact — so one component of 40 went wholly to validation. Val therefore holds 1 fold-matched negative against test's 10. The asymmetry is not sampling luck; it is a determinate consequence of a homology-dense family meeting a homology-aware split.

The defensible claim. "The probe degrades more gracefully as negatives get harder" — not "the probe is uniformly better". On validation the two are within 0.006 AU-PRC of each other.
09

What this does not show

  • Not remote-homolog detection. 283 of 320 PET sequences fall into a single homology component; 45% of all positive–positive pairs are ≥30% identical. This is within-family discrimination.
  • Against fold-matched esterases the advantage is much smaller. Decomposing the test set by what the negatives actually are, positives fixed at 42: all 62 negatives 0.9681; the 36 naive controls 1.0000; the 3 tRNA entries 1.0000; the 23 wrong-fold EC 3.1.1 0.9141; the 10 α/β-hydrolase-fold negatives 0.8167 (95% CI 0.676–0.926). 39 of 62 test negatives are separated perfectly, so the headline is a weighted blend in which most of the weight is free. Much of it is separating hydrolases from non-hydrolases.
  • Test is spent. Final metrics were read off test; further tuning must return to validation or use a fresh set.
  • Wide intervals. 42 test positives in 20 components — the significant deltas have lower bounds of +0.006 and +0.014, close to zero.
  • No causal evidence yet about which features drive the decision — that is the Catalytic Latent Atlas, and it is correlational.
